      Isosceles Triangle Theorem

      If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles opposite them are congruent.

      AB ≅ AC → ∠B ≅ ∠C

      Converse

      If two angles of a triangle are congruent, then the sides opposite them are congruent.

      ∠B ≅ ∠C → AB ≅ AC

      How it helps a proof

      The theorem turns a pair of sides into a pair of angles. The converse turns a pair of angles into a pair of sides. Either way, you collect one more part for SSS, SAS, ASA or AAS.

      Proving the Isosceles Triangle Theorem with a reflection

      Given AB ≅ AC. Prove ∠B ≅ ∠C. Press the reflect button above to watch each step.

      1. Draw ray AD, the bisector of ∠BAC, and reflect the whole triangle across line AD.
      2. A is on the line of reflection, so A stays where it is.
      3. Reflections keep angle measures. ∠BAD ≅ ∠CAD, so ray AB lands on ray AC.
      4. Reflections keep lengths. AB ≅ AC, so B lands exactly on C (and C lands on B).
      5. The reflection maps △ABC onto △ACB, so ∠ABC lands on ∠ACB. Rigid motions keep angle measures, so ∠B ≅ ∠C.

      The same fold proves it with congruent triangles too: AB ≅ AC (Given), ∠BAD ≅ ∠CAD (Definition of angle bisector), AD ≅ AD (Reflexive), so △ABD ≅ △ACD by SAS, and ∠B ≅ ∠C because corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent.

      Congruent means one figure can be moved exactly onto the other. Translations, rotations and reflections are rigid motions: they never change a length or an angle. To prove SAS, we show the three given parts force △DEF to land exactly on △ABC.

      SASby rigid motions

      Given AB ≅ DE, ∠A ≅ ∠D, AC ≅ DF. Prove △ABC ≅ △DEF.

        ASA the same way

        Move D to A and E to B (that uses AB ≅ DE). Now ∠D ≅ ∠A aims DF along ray AC, and ∠E ≅ ∠B aims EF along ray BC. F is on both rays, and they cross only at C.

        SSS uses isosceles

        Move D to A and E to B. If F is on the other side, C and F are both AC from A and BC from B. So A and B each sit on the perpendicular bisector of CF, which makes line AB that bisector. Reflecting across AB sends F to C.

        Why the rules are theorems

        The definition of congruence is “a rigid motion maps one onto the other.” SSS, SAS, ASA and AAS are shortcuts: they prove such a motion exists without checking all six parts.

        Set up the proof. Every proof follows the same plan: the intro lists the givens, the body uses properties and theorems to collect the three parts, and the conclusion is what you are proving. Correct steps get marked on the diagram so you can watch the three parts appear.

        Before you write a single line

        1. Mark the givens on the diagram.
        2. Look for free parts: a shared side (Reflexive Property) or vertical angles.
        3. Find one more part from a definition or theorem: midpoint, bisector, perpendicular, parallel lines.
        4. Two congruent sides in one triangle? The angles opposite them are congruent (Isosceles Triangle Theorem). Two congruent angles? Use the converse to get sides.
        5. Name the three parts in order around the triangle. That order names the rule.
        6. Write the conclusion with matching vertices in matching positions.
